Topic: Proxies for scientific accomplishment

Morgan Rogers (he/him) (Aug 31 2026 at 15:42):

Following Beppe Metere's post linked in #community: discussion > AI for mathematical research @ 💬 , I wonder if anyone has ideas for reliable measures of scientific ability / integrity / accomplishment that could plausibly come to replace the detestable bibliographic metrics that are the current default.

An extant competing one, with its own problems, is that of direct peer evaluation: in order to determine the quality of a candidate, one asks other experts in their field to evaluate them. That system:

  • Relies on an implicit or explicit community of trusted experts to draw from.
  • If the expert knows the candidate directly, there is risk of bias one way or the other, and a power relationship to be exploited.
  • If they don't, they will have to fall back on scientific production anyway, although one can hope that an expert won't merely look at bibliometrics...

It isn't terrible, but I can think of plenty of research that hasn't been picked up because this system incentivises experts to support work close to their own rather than a diversity of topics, since this strengthens their position in the community.

Amar Hadzihasanovic (Aug 31 2026 at 18:38):

Something that seems to be a pattern about metrics is that they are quite reliable (and interchangeable) in recognising "insufficient" candidates who do not meet the minimal requirements, but arbitrary and unstable in the ranking and evaluation of "sufficient" candidates. I have long thought that there is a good argument for two-stage selection processes where any reasonable method is used to identify all the candidates that meet the requirements, and then a lottery is used to pick among those.

Beppe Metere (Sep 01 2026 at 13:28):

Hi @Morgan Rogers (he/him) . Indeed, direct peer evaluation certainly has problems, including biases, personal relations, and power dynamics. Nevertheless, in the present situation I think it remains more reliable than bibliometric evaluation, especially when it is conducted with rigor and evaluators are required to explain their choices.
Furthermore, LLMs are making bibliometric indicators even less reliable. They can increase the production of papers, citations, and variations of existing results without a corresponding increase in scientific understanding. Bibliometric indicators were already indirect measures of quality; now the distance between scientific output and scientific value can become even larger.
Of course, peer evaluation is not objective. Experts can favour areas, methods, and communities close to their own. But I think that trying to automate and objectivise evaluation is illusory. Every evaluation involves choices about what is important, original, or promising. An algorithm does not eliminate these choices: it only hides them. We need evaluators who explain their judgements and assume responsibility for them. This is something LLM cannot do: taking responsibility of their own choices.
The fact that some areas of mathematics try to prevail over others, sometimes suffocating them, is a political problem. It cannot be solved simply by changing the evaluation method. If we want to preserve a diversity of topics and approaches, we must make explicit choices of scientific policy.
The problem is then moved: if we reject superficial bibliometric evaluation, what should we evaluate?
Perhaps we should discuss directly with candidates their research, objectives, methods, and future plans, including how they use LLMs and how they verify their results. We could also evaluate mathematical fluency: the capacity to move between definitions, examples, proofs, and intuitions; to recognise incomplete arguments; to formulate good questions; and to explain why a problem or result is significant.
This leads to a more general question: should we search for mathematicians with exactly the same competences as those who entered academia before LLMs? Some capacities remain essential, such as conceptual understanding, critical judgement, and verification. But the intelligent and responsible use of LLMs may also become part of mathematical competence.
The question may therefore be less whether a candidate produced a paper without assistance, and more whether they understand and control the mathematical process, can verify what has been produced, and can defend their scientific choices.

Snow Walker (Sep 01 2026 at 15:13):

Without significant effort, an AI is still not capable of writing an innovative Annals or Acta paper : they can give interesting examples, but most of the AI results I've seen have not been especially innovative in terms of method, they just connect things that already exist in the literature in a superhuman way.

It further goes without saying that even if AI competely takes over, a talented mathematician armed with AI is always going to ask better questions than an worse mathematician with the same AI. The problem solving gets pushed one level up. There's a reason why many of the striking results so far have been found by Levent Alpöge, a man who was an one of the most talented young mathematicians in the world before going to Anthropic.

The solution is to double down on what has always been valued in mathematics, quality over quantity. If every article below, say, the usual standards of, say, JPAA can be automated, perhaps we need to appropiately decrease the value of lower publications. It is already the case that in many areas of mathematics, publications in weak journals "don't count."

Direct peer evaluation is just a terrible way to do things. "Peer" here usually means unaccountable older person at some prestigious university who already has too much direct power over publication and hiring decisions, and is often much weaker than the young people in the field because they got their job in the 1970/80s in a less competitive time. I can think of at least one case where the old guard has treated a talented young mathematician, who was, if publication record reflects anything, much more distinguished than them, quite poorly because, as it appears to me, they didn't like their attitude and lack of deference.

Nicolai Kraus (Sep 02 2026 at 19:29):

Here are my two cents on this. To me it seems that, when hiring PhD students and postdocs, decisions are generally very good. That's because experts spend a significant amount of time and effort to choose people in their own field. It's only at faculty level when decisions become based on questionable criteria, because people from other areas have no idea how to evaluate candidates, and maybe also don't have the time that would be needed. (Instead of "don't have the time" one could say "don't have the motivation", which would suggest that panel members are just too lazy to evaluate applications properly, but I think that would be a bit unfair -- developing enough insight about all the fields of all the applicants to come to fair conclusions just isn't possible.) Anyway, that does suggest that direct peer evaluation could work better than what is currently used. (I'm personally reasonably sure that it would work better than what is currently used, but I can't prove it.)

Unfortunately, even if better than what is currently used, direct peer evaluation is still quite difficult and potentially unfair when one has to compare people from very different areas, and I think all the issues that previous posters have mentioned are real issues. It also would make conferences quite a bit different, in a bad way. I'm in favour of what @Amar Hadzihasanovic (#community: discussion > Proxies for scientific accomplishment @ 💬) suggested:

  1. Use metrics to filter out applicants who're definitely not good enough. Because, let's face it, if someone has never found a result or written a paper that at least some people in their own community cared about, then they're not a strong candidate using any reasonable decision system.
  2. Use random chance to rank the "good" candidates.

Would that mean that the best people get hired? Of course not, because of the second step. But actually think that the luck one would need would be no more than the luck required in the current system, and I don't think the system would be worse than what we currently have. A main positive effect would be that people could stop gaming metrices and do what they actually want to do in their job. I also think the same principle would be suitable for grant application decisions and similar things that are too difficult to evaluate properly.

I agree with most things that @Snow Walker wrote above, but this bit seems unfair to the "unaccountable older person[s]":

unaccountable older person at some prestigious university who already has too much direct power over publication and hiring decisions, and is often much weaker than the young people in the field because they got their job in the 1970/80s in a less competitive time.

I think this is unfair because:
a) Formulated as "having too much direct power" makes it sound like a privilege, but it's real work that someone has to do [at least in the current system], and it's not something that mathematicians actually want to do.
b) My impression is that many people who're nowadays professors are worse (or at least aren't better) mathematicians than they were when they were postdocs. Apart from the standard explanation about brain aging, I think there's also the effect that, as a postdoc, one has much more time to focus on maths, while afterwards, one is forced to do all sorts of staff that one didn't sign up for, which can make it challenging to keep up. As a result, it would be unreasonable to expect that current professors are better mathematicians than current postdocs who aspire to become professors. Peva Blanchard (Sep 02 2026 at 20:44):

We are not very far from Goodhart's law. So any explicitly stated rule will almost surely find an adversarial counter-example.

From what I understand so far:

  • bibliometrics are good at recognising bad candidates, but are bad at recognising good ones
  • lottery, as a means to select among good candidates, treats every one equally, but is likely to miss the best
  • direct peer evaluation leverages social credit, but correlating social acceptance with being good is debatable

Peva Blanchard (Sep 02 2026 at 20:47):

Maybe, another perspective on this topic is to emphasize the properties of the recruitment process that we'd find desirable. Here are a few on top of my head:

  • candidates should be told beforehand about the steps of the recruitment process
  • the recruitment process involves a deliberation, the trace of which can be communicated to the candidate
  • the recruitment process itself can be debated and updated (in one's organization)

The last one is probably my favorite, because this is what enables improving based on learned lessons. But this raises questions of governance.

Amar Hadzihasanovic (Sep 03 2026 at 05:27):

Re: the lottery system, I think in general it would be healthy if calls for applications were treated like "procurements": the university / the funding body is in need of these services, and is looking for candidates who are able to provide them. Once it is established that a group of candidates is capable of providing those services, each one of them is equally suited to getting the job / the funding, and it is irrelevant (with respect to the call) how they perform outside of that domain.

Matteo Capucci (he/him) (Sep 03 2026 at 15:13):

I recently learned of a Finnish tech startup's hiring process where (after a modicum of filtering) they just pay the candidate to show up and work with them for a week.

Eventually, all metrics are proxies for 'how well could we work with this person to deliver outstanding research?'. If someone shows up for a few days, gives a seminar, has conversations with peopl, then the group can _really_ feel in their gut if they would be a good fit or not.

The only downsides of this approach I can think of are (1) economical (you must pay travel for 1 week, or worse, ask the candidate to foot the bill) and (2) some people (I'm thinking some neurodivergent folks especially) might feel uncomfortable into such a situation of scrutiny.
