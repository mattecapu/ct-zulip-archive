The three distance theorem (Steinhaus conjecture, proved in the 1950s by Sós, Świerczkowski, and Surányi independently): take any irrational α\alphaα and place the points {α},{2α},…,{Nα}\{\alpha\}, \{2\alpha\}, \ldots, \{N\alpha\}{α},{2α},…,{Nα} on the circle R/Z\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}R/Z . The gaps between consecutive points take on at most 3 distinct lengths. And when there are exactly 3 lengths, one of them is the sum of the other two.

The reason I think this might excite you: it's a theorem about 1-dimensional Diophantine approximation on the circle, and the three lengths are governed by the continued fraction expansion of α\alphaα . The transition from 3 lengths to 2 happens exactly when NNN is a denominator of a convergent of α\alphaα .

All this makes sense to me. I checked the main claim in an example that's important in music theory - and it works, and indeed explains a lot of stuff.

I am sure any program launched by these, the most famously exploitative math publishers, is cleverly designed to benefit them. So you should think about how this is benefiting them.

For example, if you make contact with them, they will be tracking you. Here is a bit about how Elsevier does that:

The term "surveillance publishing" was coined by Jefferson Pooley (Muhlenberg College) in a 2022 paper in the Journal of Electronic Publishing. His central example is Elsevier, and the picture is quite concrete. Here are some of the main threads:

The "full-stack" data harvest. Elsevier has pursued a research-lifecycle data-harvesting strategy, aiming to develop and sell prediction products to universities and other customers. (See University of Michigan.) Through acquisitions and product launches, Elsevier has positioned itself to collect behavioral data at every stage of the research process — from lab notebooks through to impact scoring. (See The n-Category Café.) The acquisitions include Mendeley (reference manager), Pure (research information management), SciVal (researcher performance metrics), Plum Analytics (altmetrics), and Scopus, among others.

ScienceDirect as a tracking platform. A 2023 SPARC report by Becky Yoose documented the specifics of how ScienceDirect actually works under the hood. They found that ScienceDirect uses web beacons, cookies, and other invasive web surveillance methods to track user behavior outside and beyond the ScienceDirect website itself. (See the University of Nebraska-Lincoln.) The platform extensively collects personal data — behavioral and location data — from ScienceDirect and combines it with personal data harvested from third-party sources, including other RELX subsidiaries and data brokers. Third parties such as Google, Adobe, Cloudflare, and New Relic also collect personal data through extensive use of trackers embedded in the ScienceDirect site.

The RELX/LexisNexis connection. This is arguably the most alarming part. Elsevier is a subsidiary of RELX, which is also a leading data broker and provider of "risk" products that offer expansive databases of personal information to corporations, governments, and law enforcement agencies. (See Zenodo.) The SPARC report raised the concern that personal data gathered through academic platforms could potentially flow into these surveillance and data brokering products — and their analysis noted that privacy policies can be changed unilaterally, and verbal denials of specific data uses are not legally binding. (See the University of Nebraska-Lincoln.)

Cross-product data sharing. Elsevier's own privacy policy states that if you create a personal account on ScienceDirect, Scopus, Mendeley, ClinicalKey, or other Elsevier services, they share your usage activity, preferences, and other information across all these services. (See Elsevier.) So your Mendeley library informs what ScienceDirect shows you, and vice versa.

The Enhanced PDF viewer was also flagged in the n-Category Café discussion — Tom Leinster noted that Elsevier's Enhanced PDF viewer reportedly tracks where you click and view, and pointed out that this is yet another incentive to get papers from the arXiv or Sci-Hub instead. (See The n-Category Café.)

As Pooley frames it, the profits from Elsevier's legacy publishing business — built on scholars' unpaid labor — have financed this surveillance apparatus, and now Elsevier is also skimming the behavioral data and selling that too. The core idea is that publishers aren't just middlemen anymore; they're using their position at every stage of the research lifecycle to extract behavioral data and sell predictive analytics back to the very universities that are already paying for subscriptions and APCs.

The question was, suppose you are given a finite presentation of a monoid and you know that the monoid generated by the presentation is not infinite. Is the equational theory decidable?

Answer: it is obviously semi decidable whether two expressions agree because you can search for a proof that they agree. So it suffices to prove it is semi decidable whether they are not equal.

Because you know the monoid is finite, it suffices to enumerate all finite Cayley tables representing a monoid and enumerate all homomorphisms into that Cayley table (a map from the generators into the Cayley table can be checked to satisfy finitely many relations in finite time). If you find a homomorphism from the free monoid where the terms are distinguished by the homomorphism then they are not equal.

Beppe Metere (Sep 08 2026 at 15:05):

The facts concerning this dispute still need to be clarified. However, independently of what will finally emerge, this episode has already shown a structural problem that goes much beyond.

The companies developing frontier AI systems are playing several roles at the same time. They provide researchers with increasingly essential tools, but then they keep their prompts, drafts and failed attempts on their servers. Moreover, they decide how much computational power each user can access; and, at the same time, they may compete with those researchers for the same scientific results.

This is clearly not a good situation for science. If access to the most advanced models becomes necessary in order to make progress in mathematics, physics or biology, the companies controlling these models will also have an enormous influence on the production of scientific knowledge. They can decide who receives early access, who obtains more computational resources, which universities become privileged partners and which countries, institutions or individual researchers remain excluded. Prices, usage limits and corporate policies may end up shaping the direction of scientific research as much as universities and public funding agencies do.

The Alpöge–Buckmaster affair should therefore be taken as a warning. AI is quickly becoming a fundamental infrastructure for scientific research, but this infrastructure remains almost completely private, centralised and opaque. Public research institutions risk becoming dependent on companies whose commercial interests do not necessarily coincide with the principles of open research, confidentiality and correct scientific attribution. If AI is going to become a fundamental instrument of science, advanced models and the computational resources necessary to use them should become part of a public, open and internationally accessible research infrastructure.

I read his older paper where he introduces the formalism about the tasks of production. He gave two lectures about it, part 1 and part 2.

He focuses more on automation than AI specifically in this work. Very briefly,

  • He poses that the production (i.e. added value produced over a year) is the result of multiple tasks
  • Each task is assigned to labor (e.g. manual work) or capital (e.g. machine work)
  • Automation is about moving existing tasks from labor to capital
  • And new tasks (labor) can appear

He studies how the share of labor in the production behaves as automation increases or new tasks arrive.

  • when automation increases, there are two competing effects: productivity effect (positive) and displacement effect (negative)
  • when new tasks are created, there two positive effects: productivity effect and reinstatement effect

One interesting perspective he lays out is the distinction between brilliant and so-so technologies. A so-so technology is one that works ok but is not good enough for its productivity effect to compensate the displacement effect.

I think a lot of mathematicians (high-profile or not) have done a good job of explaining the damage AI can do to mathematics from an internal point of view, but our struggle might benefit from looking outwards a bit more: there is relatively little discussion of what mathematics contributes to the other sciences and to society more generally, and of what we risk losing through the increasing use of LLMs as a substitute for human mathematical enquiry. In a context where a large number of scientists and engineers (not to mention the general public) already see mathematics merely as a big toolbox with ready-made solutions for their favourite problems, LLMs create the illusion of being a general-purpose tool that allows them to dispense with the toolbox altogether. And given a lot of people's experience with maths (in school, university...) I imagine quite a few of them are delighted by that prospect! There is an interesting clash of paradigms here: machine learning is largely founded on the idea that predictive ability is all that matters, independently of our ability to explain a given phenomenon. However, even if prediction is a central part of science, it is not the whole story. This is something we need to argue more pedagogically as mathematicians (the burden should not just fall on us of course, but we appear to be some of the first in line for these AI companies' attacks on science, so let us also be the first to reiterate the value of actually understanding stuff!).

Imho, the core argument should be that prediction without understanding is myopic and unsustainable---ultimately, there is no such thing as prediction-only science. For the same reasons, there is no viable substitute for a solid grasp of the mathematical methods one uses. A quick look at the large number of bogus results caused by a misunderstanding of statistics (I am thinking, for example, of the reproducibility crises in biology, medicine, social sciences, etc.) should make it clear that choosing the right tool and applying it correctly require a level of understanding for which current LLMs are no substitute. "Current LLMs", as someone will surely object, but soon the models will be capable of ensuring that mathematical tools are properly applied to any scientific question of interest(!), and all any scientist or engineer will have to do is provide enough context for them to select and apply the right method. Perhaps... But this strikes me as a much more difficult problem than producing mathematical proofs. Ensuring that a number (or any other mathematical abstraction) actually means something, out there in the real-world, is not something whose coherence can be checked in Lean! It is an intentional activity that one might call "meta-rational", a form of know-how which I doubt could (and should) be fully automated. (In much the same way that meaning-making activities inherent to mathematics, such as choosing which research directions to explore, which concepts to define, which problem to tackle... also escape mechanisation.)

In summary, I believe we should be better at arguing for the value of mathematical understanding outside of mathematics, beyond formal problem-solving. What do we really mean when we say that a human proof of a given mathematical theorem is better than a vibe-coded LLM proof? It is more or less clear to mathematicians, but I think we also need good answers to this question that make sense to other scientists and, ultimately, everyone else. Ironically, this might require a better of understanding of understanding itself, by engaging explicitly (and humbly) with fields that have much to teach us in this respect, like philosophy, pedagogy, and the social sciences.

Of course, this will also require us to rethink the current political economy of mathematical academia; as many others have pointed out, in this respect, AI companies are merely exploiting existing fault lines in the way that the mathematical community attributes merit, jobs, and funding (and note that changing the latter requires arguing for the value of human-centered mathematics beyond mathematics, as I am exhorting us to do here, since mathematics is in competition for funding with other fields, sadly).

  • I am assuming that AI agents have become basically as intelligent as us, so they reliably "know" when they have shared original work with someone, at least in the great majority of cases, just as I know when I contributed novel work in a discussion with someone and I should be listed as an author of a paper coming out of that discussion (or not).
  • Of course the agents could also go crazy (misalignment) and store songs, novels, theorems in the blockchain which they never shared with anyone. Or share some wonderful original insight with a user and not record it. The point is that this misbehavior is easily detectable, as long as it happens frequently enough (otherwise it would't be a problem): just ask, at regular intervals, agents to come up with new stuff and check that they actually write it in the log, or periodically monitor user activity and agent logging and check that they are consistent (like, there is no overproduction of logs, a sign that AI is making stuff up).
  • The main purpose of the material stored in the blockchain is to serve as "negative evidence" that some piece of intellectual work is human-made. For example, a person can go to a notary with their creation, the notary can check the creation against the ledger and officially declare that nothing similar to it is present in it, so no compliant AI generated it. Or some other mechanism, or the ledger is public, I don't know. Anyway, in the extreme case that every AI agent available in the world is compliant, this gives almost certain proof that the work is human-made. In the other extreme case, if very few AI agents are compliant, this is very weak evidence, almost useless.
  • In light of all the confusion generated by my use of the word "copyright", it is important to stress that the information contained in the ledger has basically no value. It is provably AI-generated, and it either already appears somewhere in some form (because the user who obtained it published it, necessarily acknowledging AI use, as they would never be able to claim human-made status except through a crooked notary etc., but then any other notary could easily reveal the scam), or it was never used but provably "belongs to AI", which means that it does not belong to a person or moral entity, so it may not be copyrighted in the usual sense of the word and monetized in any way. This addresses one point made by @V Slicer in their last post.
