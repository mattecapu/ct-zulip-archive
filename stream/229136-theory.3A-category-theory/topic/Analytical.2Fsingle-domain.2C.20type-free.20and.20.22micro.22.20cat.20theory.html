Topic: Analytical/single-domain, type-free and "micro" cat theory

William Oakcraft (Nov 21 2026 at 21:26):

Good afternoon! Just some far-out (and possibly foolish) curiosities I have always had and I would like to hear your opinions and suggestions (of references, similar reflections or proposals).

I think one main trope of the more hard-analytic style in mathematics is the work inside only one object (usually the real or complex field with continuous and differentiable functions, Hausdorff topology, some usual norms or metrics) in a stereotypical "type-free" manner with an abundance of operations being defined as well as a lot of freedom to define new operations (and, in the more applied environments - especially in mathematical physics - sometimes little care if these operations are defined everywhere or have good desirable properties). Through my experience in numerical analysis and other applied areas I have come to feel that even any worry about exact solutions is thrown out the window (and thus I came to appreciate a lot projects such as Bacci-Mardare-Plotkin quantitative equational theories trying to make this bridge).

Usually it's argued a lot (especially in philosophy environments) that one could make do all mathematics in a similar "single-object/type-free/single-domain" style with no need for any stratification, sorting or division of the domain. However, outside of very special (although very popular) such "analytical"/applied research programs and styles, it seems as long as any abstraction is introduced mathematical work inherently gains a "typed" feeling where a more structural foundation would seem much more natural (and then set theoretic and similar type-free/unsorted foundations gain only a merely formal/symbolic status, of interest to model theorist but don't reflecting much the actual style mathematicians work with).

From my experience with general logic and diagonalization/self-referent phenomena literature though it also seems most of the (formal, metatheoretical) problems that a more typed/structural foundation aims to solve usually are born out of "bad too strong defaults" (usually in the deductive system) to begin with, and even in typed/structural settings these problems never seem to go away completely. On the other hand solutions to diagonalization phenomena that either 1) weaken structural rules (the substructural route - in affine/linear logics), 2) embrace partiality and finitism (here partiality meaning either in term denotation but also weaker notions of closure for operations and completeness - I think bounded arithmetic/bounded logics but also Dan Willard's self-verifying systems - from memory I think Paul C. Gilmore and also Vopenka proposed many such systems) or 3) abandon equality and strong equivalence relations as "the default" entirely (Feferman but especially Makkai - even though Makkai work in typed settings and with a structural style) but I've never actually seen any more "synthetic" (and especially categorial) mathematics done in such typically extremely analytic/type-free/single-domain style. Actually one of the central advantages and "dogmas" usually pointed out in the categorial/synthetic style is its structuralism, and from day one when I first got in contact with category theory I've always asked how it would look like without the reliance on the typing of its objects, as if it was "more analytic" (the first chapter of Freyd/Scedrov's Categories, Allegories gives a glimpse of that).

Meanwhile the benefits of the approximate and "don't mind the denotation of terms" character of more applied analytical areas can be glimpsed in results such as Fiore/Leinster's Objects of Categories as Complex Numbers, and after rediscovering the original (extremely analytic) style of Boole's Algebra of Logic program I've always contemplated how modern "synthetic/discrete" mathematics and logic could look like if not for De Morgan/Schroder/Peirce and Frege's later criticisms and reframing of this program and of mathematics with an obsession for denotating terms, closure, "closed" minimalist structures, clear internal/external borders, size issues etc.

Do any of you have ever had a similar feeling, curiosity or have some intuition into this? Is structuralism and "typing", at least in the superficial level (as a tool for the mathematician, not in foundations), indispensable for work in categorial/"synthetic"/typed mathematics as we see today? Are there any works or proposals (obscure papers, theses, anything) you think are going in a similar direction I am trying to convey?

Mind that I am not talking about "foundations/logic for logicians", but the actual tools the mathematician uses. I have strong suspicions that proof styles/styles of mathematical work which such different foundational premises haven't actually got more popular because of the historical connection between some popular notations and some strong presuppositions most people have about them (mostly in equational/calculational/algebraic/chain-of-equations style proofs, where the the rewriting relation is always supposed to be a very strong equality-like one - with Leibniz/"equals-for-equals" principle and other very liberal properties -, it must be a single one - and not multiple rewriting relations -, that the terms must be denoting and singular/uniquely denoting etc - but also the "typing-as-membership" paradigm that usually leads to very stratified type structures - compare this to mereology or of the typing notation as a pre- or partial order, with either a flattened type structure or with "singleton constructors" as in Joyal's ZF-algebras).

My last question/curiosity, which could be even more foolish but I still would like your thoughts: the usual intuitive conception of almost all foundational structures one may find (set hierarchies, type hierarchies, n-categories - one could say even metatheory/language hierarchies) are usually based on that of the natural numbers (or the usual set-theoretical hierarchies); i.e. increasingly "higher" and more abstract totalities. Of course that may be just a matter of perspective and intuition (as one could also see these "increasing" "ever higher" hierarchies as being actually "decreasing" "zooms") but does anyone know of any proposal or have any intuition if it would make sense to have such a foundational structure go in the other more "infinitesimal" direction? (as in assuming a closed totality with a given structure and conducting research in ever "smaller" fragments/parts of it). This always felt like a natural idea for me when I first got in contact with both coalgebras and refutational/bidirectional logics/deductive systems. I imagined: the usual way one thinks about a rewriting systems is the classical "iterative/constructive" conception of "building everything stepwise from a few primitives"; this is great for "implemented compsci" and applied mathematics but usually more pure mathematics is carried would in the specification-level (comprehension principles/object-satisfies-property), and this would be a lot closer to a "refutational" rewriting system. Does anyone know of such a thing or have any idea into it?

I apologize both for the long text but also the purposefully open-ended and maybe vague questions; this is supposed to be a quasi-philosophical heavily speculative question which probably wouldn't be welcomed into forums such as MathOverflow or StackExchange. Mind that I am not trying to make any arguments or defend any stances, I am just curious and I think maybe getting in contact with such radically alternative formulations of subjects I work with could be very productive.

I much appreciate your thoughts and suggestions and I wish everyone a great weekend :))

William
