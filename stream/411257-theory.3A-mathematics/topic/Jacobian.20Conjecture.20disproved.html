Topic: Jacobian Conjecture disproved

John Baez (Jul 20 2026 at 08:33):

The Jacobian conjecture is a crazy relative of the inverse function theorem.

The inverse function says that if F:Rn→RnF: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^nF:Rn→Rn is a smooth function and its matrix of partial deriatives ∂Fi/∂xj\partial F_i /\partial x_j∂Fi​/∂xj​ is invertible at some point, FFF has a smooth inverse in some neighborhood of that point.

The Jacobi Conjecture says that if F:Cn→CnF: \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^nF:Cn→Cn is a polynomial function and its matrix of partial deriatives ∂Fi/∂xj\partial F_i /\partial x_j∂Fi​/∂xj​ is invertible at every point, FFF has a polynomial inverse.

This seems inherently implausible, but it was open since 1939, resisting attempts to find a counterexample... until yesterday.

John Baez (Jul 20 2026 at 08:35):

The mathematician Levent Alpöge, who works for Anthropic, found a counterexample to the Jacobi conjecture using Claude Fable. It's easy enough to check:

F(𝑥,𝑦,𝑧) = (𝑦²(3𝑥𝑦+4)(𝑥𝑦+1)+𝑧(𝑥𝑦+1)³, 3𝑥𝑦²(3𝑥𝑦+4)+3𝑥𝑧(𝑥𝑦+1)²+𝑦, 2𝑥−𝑥³𝑧−3𝑥²𝑦)

has Jacobian determinant equal to -2 everywhere, but it's not invertible since

F(0, 0, -1/4) = F(1, -3/2, 13/2) = F(-1, 3/2, 13/2) = (-1/4, 0, 0)

John Baez (Jul 20 2026 at 11:30):

Oh, okay. I thought Fosco was talking about Fable's solution. Yeah, the conjecture itself seems inherently implausible in a naive prima facie way, not based on any deep knowledge of algebraic geometry.

Why should local invertibility imply global invertibility? For example, everyone learns in school an analytic map f:C→Cf : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}f:C→C such that the determinant of its Jacobian is invertible everywhere, so fff is locally one-to-one, but fff fails to be one-to-one. But the Jacobian conjecture says this can't happen for polynomials. So somehow the magic would need to come from replacing "analytic" by "polynomial".

Of course, a lot of magic comes from replacing "analytic" by "polynomial". So maybe if I actually understood algebraic geometry, I'd know something that makes the Jacobian conjecture seem plausible. But I don't, so it always seemed amazing-if-true.

John Baez (Jul 20 2026 at 14:31):

I hadn't even known about the Hadamard conjecture:

For every integer kkk there's a 4k×4k4k \times 4k4k×4k matrix whose entries are either +1 or −1 and whose rows are mutually orthogonal.

Wikipedia says that in 2014, there were 12 multiples of 4 less than 2000 for which no Hadamard matrix of that order was known.[9] They are: 668, 716, 892, 1132, 1244, 1388, 1436, 1676, 1772, 1916, 1948, and 1964.

So, the easiest thing for a LLM to try might be to find a Hadamard matrix of order 668. Proving that none exists could be hard.

Unlike the Jacobian conjecture, where the counterexample was very easy to check, a counterexample to the Hadamard conjecture is a statement that at least 2668×6682^{668 \times 668}2668×668 matrices don't have rows that are mutually orthogonal!
