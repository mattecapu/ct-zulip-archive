Matteo Capucci (he/him) said:

What do you mean by an 'admissibility constraint'?

By “admissibility” I mean admissibility relative to extra coherence conditions imposed on a functor.

Suppose we have two categories A and B and a functor

F: A -> B.

As an ordinary functor, F sends every object and morphism of A to B. But now suppose we require F to be symmetric monoidal. Then we also require coherent structure maps like

F(X) tensor F(Y) ~= F(X tensor Y)

and

1_B ~= F(1_A),

with the usual compatibility conditions for associativity, units, and symmetry.

So the issue is not whether an object or morphism has an image under F. The issue is whether a proposed collection of structures in A can be carried to B in a way compatible with all of those extra coherence conditions.

That is what I mean by admissible: a structure is admissible if its image can participate in the full symmetric-monoidal structure of F.

So, schematically:

ordinary functorial image
vs.
image compatible with the required coherence.

Equivalently, some data may map perfectly well under the underlying functor, but fail to extend to a symmetric-monoidal realization because the tensor products, symmetries, or compositions cannot all be made compatible at once.

Let me try to define the language problem mathematically:

For language, the mathematical problem is not merely to define an interpretation functor. It is to determine which syntactically valid expressions remain semantically admissible once interpreted meanings impose type and consistency constraints.

A concise formulation is:

Given a syntactic category C_syn, a semantic category C_sem, an interpretation functor

F: C_syn -> C_sem,

and a category of consistent semantic typings

p: E_cons -> C_sem,

determine the subcategories D of C_syn for which the restricted interpretation

F|D: D -> C_sem

admits a lift

F_hat_D: D -> E_cons

with

p o F_hat_D = F|D.

Then study the admissibility poset

A = { D subseteq C_syn : F|D admits such a lift }.

The core questions are:

  • when does a global lift exist on all of C_syn?
  • if not, do maximal admissible subcategories exist?
  • is there a unique largest admissible subcategory?
  • how can it be constructed from local semantic typing constraints?
  • what conditions guarantee closure of admissibility under composition?

In plain language:

The problem is to determine the largest fragment of syntactically valid language whose semantic interpretation can be assigned mutually compatible meanings everywhere at once.

Hi @John Dai,

I believe I understand what you are after, so I will try to help formalize your idea.

In particular, I think the following intuition you expressed can be made mathematically precise:

This led me to the tentative idea that coherence may sometimes play the role of an admissibility constraint rather than merely a consistency condition.

and similarly:

Can coherence itself function as an admissibility constraint, rather than merely as a consistency condition?

First, every monoid can be viewed as a category with a single object. Using this, I will denote the multiplicative monoid Z/2Z={0,1}\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}=\{0,1\}Z/2Z={0,1} by Csem\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{sem}}Csem​, referring to the semantic category that you mentioned.

Then I will denote by W\mathcal{W}W the list of words in my language, say a tokenization of English, and by ΣW\Sigma\mathcal{W}ΣW the free monoid generated by W\mathcal{W}W.

For example,

W={"An"," ant"," can"," eat"," a"," universe",…}.\mathcal{W}=\{\texttt{"An"},\texttt{" ant"},\texttt{" can"},\texttt{" eat"},\texttt{" a"},\texttt{" universe"},\ldots\}.W={"An"," ant"," can"," eat"," a"," universe",…}.

Because each of these words makes sense individually, we can assign a valid semantic value to each of these atomic syntactic components:

F("An")=1,F(" ant")=1,…F(\texttt{"An"})=1,\quad F(\texttt{" ant"})=1,\quad\dotsF("An")=1,F(" ant")=1,…

However, making the mapping FFF preserve the underlying monoid structures becomes more difficult as we move from these local pieces toward more global expressions.

We may certainly have

F("An ant")=1,F(" can eat")=1,F(" a universe")=1,…F(\texttt{"An ant"})=1,\quad F(\texttt{" can eat"})=1,\quad F(\texttt{" a universe"})=1,\quad\dotsF("An ant")=1,F(" can eat")=1,F(" a universe")=1,…

and we can even consider intersecting segments such as

F("An ant can eat")=1,F(" can eat a universe")=1,…F(\texttt{"An ant can eat"})=1,\quad F(\texttt{" can eat a universe"})=1,\quad\dotsF("An ant can eat")=1,F(" can eat a universe")=1,…

But the multiplication 1⋅1=11\cdot 1=11⋅1=1 in Csem\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{sem}}Csem​ is not compatible with the complete local-to-global transition when we build the larger sentence, since we would want

F("An ant can eat a universe")=0.F(\texttt{"An ant can eat a universe"})=0.F("An ant can eat a universe")=0.

I think this is very close to what you mean when you say that locally admissible structures may fail to remain admissible once they are considered as part of a larger family.

This is where categories become useful, because they allow us to restrict the monoid multiplication to sparser compositions.

It is possible to define a category C\mathcal{C}C whose set of objects is ΣW\Sigma\mathcal{W}ΣW and whose arrows are triples in ΣW×3\Sigma\mathcal{W}^{\times 3}ΣW×3, where

(m,a,b):a→b(m,a,b):a\to b(m,a,b):a→b

is an arrow in C\mathcal{C}C.

Composition is defined by

(n,b,c)∘(m,a,b)=(m⋅n,a,c).(n,b,c)\circ(m,a,b)=(m\cdot n,a,c).(n,b,c)∘(m,a,b)=(m⋅n,a,c).

("An ant","An"," can"):"An"→" can"(\texttt{"An ant"},\texttt{"An"},\texttt{" can"}):\texttt{"An"}\to\texttt{" can"}("An ant","An"," can"):"An"→" can"

(" can eat"," can"," a"):" can"→" a".(\texttt{" can eat"},\texttt{" can"},\texttt{" a"}):\texttt{" can"}\to\texttt{" a"}.(" can eat"," can"," a"):" can"→" a".

Their composition is therefore

("An ant can eat","An"," a"):"An"→" a".(\texttt{"An ant can eat"},\texttt{"An"},\texttt{" a"}):\texttt{"An"}\to\texttt{" a"}.("An ant can eat","An"," a"):"An"→" a".

Note: This category is inherently related to autoregressive language models because the category-theoretic target of an arrow (m,a,b):a→b(m,a,b): a \to b(m,a,b):a→b corresponds to what we call a "target" during the training of an LLM for a given prompt mmm and context aaa.

The categorical structure of C\mathcal{C}C now gives us more local control over semantics because we can control which contexts and targets are allowed.
