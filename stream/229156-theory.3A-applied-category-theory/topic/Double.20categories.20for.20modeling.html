John Baez (Sep 27 2026 at 14:28):

The first approach they call "ontological", a scary word - but it simply means we're saying what there is in the system, e.g. a resistor, inductor, capacitor and current source (voltage source?) connected by wires. They give examples of different kinds of ontological models - electric circuit diagrams are just one, of course. For each kind, we also get to define homomorphisms between such models.

John Baez (Sep 27 2026 at 14:31):

They then turn to "open" ontological models, and how we can use operads to describe ways of gluing together such open models. The relevant algebras of these operads are not mere structured collections of sets (as typical of operad algebras) but rather structured collections of categories, since we not only have a set of open models with some particular choice of boundary data, but a category of them.

John Baez (Sep 27 2026 at 14:41):

They conclude this section with a taste of the practical applications of these ideas: "assume-guarantee reasoning" where if you assume something about the behavior of a bunch of open systems being composed - that is, composed via some operation in some operad - you can guarantee something about the behavior of the composite system.

This is the kind of reasoning we often use when building complicated machines out of parts. It deserves to be formalized:

  • Matteo Capucci, David Jaz Myers, Compositionality of Lyapunov functions via assume-guarantee reasoning.

John Baez (Sep 27 2026 at 14:47):

Then comes a draft outline of a vision of "Mathematical modeling in the 21st century".

The first point is that we should "separate the conceptual model (the presentation or definition of the model) from the computation of the model (solving it, checking it, running it, etc.)". The work of @Nathaniel Osgood, @Evan Patterson, and many others takes this approach, calling it declarative modeling - I guess as opposed to imperative or procedural modeling.

In this approach, a model is fundamentally a mathematical object, not a piece of code saying how to compute something. (A great example is the electric circuit diagram I showed earlier.)

Declarative modelling is not quite declarative programming. Declarative programming describes a program; declarative modelling describes a model.

John Baez (Sep 27 2026 at 14:50):

However, there are many different languages for declarative modeling: not only electric circuit diagrams, but Petri nets, stock and flow models, chemical reaction networks, and many many more. Many of these languages come in large families containing many variants. To take a simple example, as soon as we create a language for describing circuits with resistors, capacitors, inductors, current sources and voltage sources, someone is going to want to add transistors. Or memristors, etcetera. There are also dozens of different widely used kinds of Petri nets.

Thus, we need a language for specifying declarative modeling languages!

This language too should be declarative, so we need to understand declarative meta-modeling.

The easiest example is perhaps a Moore machine:

  1. At left in our span of bundles we have the bundle of "changes of state". Elements of S×SS \times SS×S are pairs consisting of the state now and the next state.   2. In the middle of our span of bundles is the bundle of state-action pairs π1 ⁣:S×A→S\pi_1 \colon S \times A \to Sπ1​:S×A→S where AAA is a set of 'actions'. So, the left leg of our span is the real meat of the Moore machine.
  3. At the right of our span of bundles is the bundle of observation-action pairs π1 ⁣:O×A→O\pi_1 \colon O \times A \to Oπ1​:O×A→O where OOO is a set of observations. Fear not: a displayed category is defined to simply be a functor! But it's a functor with attitude. :laughing: (See the nLab article [[concept with attitude]] if you don't know what I mean.)

A displayed category is a functor ∂ ⁣:D→I \partial \colon D \to I∂:D→I where for each object i∈Ii \in Ii∈I, called an interface, there's a category over it, namely the fiber of ∂\partial∂ over iii.

So, it's like a fibration minus all the fancy stuff.

Spencer Breiner (Sep 30 2026 at 02:19):

John Baez said:

I found the above formalism hard to absorb not so much because a span of bundles is complicated, but because it seems concocted rather arbitrarily and the two legs of the span play very different roles. In both example I discussed, the left leg says "how a state and an action determine the future" while the right leg says "how a state determines an observation", with the action basically going along for the ride.

Part of the issue here is that the "morphism direction" here (or in lenses) doesn't coincide with the flow of information. It's best to think of left and right here as inside and outside.

The dynamics loop

  • Starts at the bottom left with an internal state.
  • This determines (or is constrained by) an observable state at the boundary.
  • ...
  • The environment responds by returning an action at the top right.
  • The right-hand (pullback) square copies this action into over the internal state.
  • Finally, the action and state together determine the next state (discrete) or flow (cts)

So information flows out, then back in, then loops to determine the dynamic. It's worth noting that the lens/span of bundles doesn't control the environmental response. That just says a component can participate in many different dynamics, depending on the behavior of it's neighbors. To fully specify a single dynamical trajectory, we have to build a closed system, which corresponds equivalently to

  • a map (lens) into the "unit" bundle y:1→1y: 1 \to 1y:1→1.
  • a section of the bundle that is the domain of the map
  • a function from observable state to compatible actions

They give lots of nice examples of cocontinuous and continuous migrations, which I will omit here. :cry:

They define a familial migration to be a functor from SetC\mathsf{Set}^{\mathsf{C}}SetC to SetD\mathsf{Set}^{\mathsf{D}}SetD that preserves connected limits.

In database-inspired jargon, a migration from SetC\mathsf{Set}^\mathsf{C}SetC to Set\mathsf{Set}Set is called a query, since an object of SetC\mathsf{Set}^\mathsf{C}SetC is an instance of a database, and you might 'query' it in some manner to get a set.

A familial migration from SetC\mathsf{Set}^\mathsf{C}SetC to Set\mathsf{Set}Set is called a duc-query because it's a disjoint unions of conjunctive queries. And clearly, if it looks like a duc and quacks like a duc, it's a duc.

An important example of a familial migration which is neither continuous nor cocontinuous is the migration sending a quiver QQQ to a new quiver Q′Q^′Q′ with the same set of vertices, but whose edges correspond to paths of edges in QQQ.

That is, the free category monad.
