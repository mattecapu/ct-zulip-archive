Stream: learning: questions

Topic: Useful models of learning?

Andrew Edelmann (Nov 17 2026 at 01:21):

This question is more about method than content. I was recently studying something in linguistics, which is basically all about the composition of various compositional systems. I started to get the impression that it would be helpful to have a compositional account of the learning process. What I have in mind is a model of the learning process where cognitive processes (self-testing, problem-solving...) and artefacts (reference resources, notes, problem sets...) come together under memory constraints to endow the learner with new knowledge and capacities. Maybe the actual structure of the thing being learned would feature in there at some level of abstraction. Such a model would be descriptive, but might implications for how one chooses to learn.

I'm wondering if anyone who is already experienced with category theory and category-theoretic modelling has anything like this that they use to inform how they learn, or think about learning. Since many of you teach category theory, maybe someone has a working model of category theory and the process of learning it that they use for teaching.

I'm also interested in applied models of learning that, say, cognitive psychologists or cognitive neuroscientists might be interested, but that's not what I'm asking about here. I'm quite new to CT, so maybe this question appears misguided to someone with stronger intuition about the subject.

I'd appreciate anyone sharing their thoughts on this.

Mods, please feel free to move if this belongs somewhere else, like meta:off-topic

John Baez (Nov 17 2026 at 10:43):

It's an interesting question: I spend a lot of time helping people learn category theory and take it quite seriously, but I've never tried to apply category theory to this endeavor.

Morgan Rogers (he/him) (Nov 17 2026 at 11:30):

I have found that knowing category theory structures my thinking/learning in a less explicit way, which one could also associate with type theory, of needing to associate to any object of study a context in which it is being studied, and to be sensitive to how the "same" object can have features which are only apparent from one context or other. There was a lengthy discussion of this phenomenon here a few years back (the recurring example was that of a sphere); maybe someone who remembers can dig it up.
That's a long way from the more concrete learning that you're interested in, though. Without pointing fingers, the attempts I have seen to apply category theory to learning/structuring thinking have tended to feel quite artificial.

PP (Nov 20 2026 at 00:38):

Learning is a kind of cognition. So, I would recommend taking a look at the literature of computational models of cognition, such as Noah D. Goodman, Joshua B. Tenenbaum & The ProbMods Contributors

Notice that all this probabilistic programming can be reformulated in terms of Markov categories.

Britt Anderson (Nov 24 2026 at 10:24):

A bit late to the exchange, but I have been interested in the benefit of applied category theory to cognitive science and cognitive psychology for a long time, and would be happy to discuss it further either here or directly via britt@uwaterloo.ca.

A few years ago I tried to drum up some interest with a workshop at the Cognitive Science Society that had Steve Phillips, Toby Smithe, and Geoffrey Cruttwell. We had a pretty full room at the start, but getting to the point where people could see the opportunities via four hours of lecture just didn't turn out to be possible, though all the experts did a really excellent job. The pedagogical challenge remains: how to help people from outside the math world find the motivation to study something for a long time with only the promise of the reward at the end. I am still looking for the "killer app" I can use to sell ACT to my community in psychology.

As further evidence of my continuing interest, Nathan Haydon and I shared a poster recently at a small meeting on computational psychology with the title: Formal Concepts, Galois Connections, and the Categorical Structure of Cognitive Explanation. Happy to expand on that if anyone is interested.

But mostly I am appending to this chat so that those with an interest in, or actual progress of, applying ACT to cognitive science know to please alert me to your work, or reach out if you want to start a discussion. Thanks.
